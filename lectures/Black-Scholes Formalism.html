  • understand the role of delta hedging;

1 The Arbitrage-free Pricing idea

1.1 Option Pricing

Suppose we want to sell a European put with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\). At maturity we would have to pay \[ (K-S_T)^+. \] where \((S_t)_{t\geq 0}\) denotes the price process of the underlying stock. At which initial price should we sell our option?

1.2 Option Pricing via expected returns?

Idea 1 (Expected Return): Make the price a function of the expected return of the stock. However, one of the striking discoveries behind the Black–Scholes theory was that this expected return disappears from the option-pricing problem.

“The warrant value did not depend on the stock’s expected return … That fascinated me.”
— Fisher Black

1.3 Option Pricing via Replication

Idea 2: Replicate the option using the stock and the money-market account:

  • At \(t=0\), invest \(\Pi_0\)

  • at \(t\geq 0\), (re)balance the portfolio with value \(\Pi\) without external cash flows, investing

\[\Delta_t \text{ in the stock and the remaining } \Pi_t-\Delta_t S_t \text{ in the money-market account.}\]

  • at \(t=T\), the strategy should replicate the option’s payoff

\[ \Pi_T = (K-S_T)^+ \quad \text{a.s.} \] If this is possible, no arbitrage implies \(\boxed{\text{Put price} = \Pi_0.}\)

1.3.1 Do such strategies exist?

1.4 Option Pricing via Replication (ctn.)

To get the idea, assume that the stock price follows a geometric Brownian motion (for simplicity) without a rate of return and zero intrerest rates, then the portfolio dynamics are \[d\Pi_t\overset{self-fin.}{=} \Delta_t dS_t\overset{.}{=} \Delta_t \sigma S_t dW_t.\] When the value process of the option is \(V(t,S_t)\) for a \(V\in C^{1,2}([0,T)\times \mathbb R)\), we find \[dV(t,S_t) \overset{\text{Ito's Lemma}}{=} \partial_t V(t,S_t) dt + \partial_s V(t,S_t) dS_t + \frac{\partial_{ss}V(t,S_t)}2 \sigma^2 S_t^2 dt\] Given the terminal condition \(V(T,S_T)= (K-S_T)^+\), this identifies \[ \begin{aligned} &V,\text{ by comparing the drifts:}\,\boxed{\partial_t V(t,S_t) + \frac{\partial_{ss}V(t,S_t)}2 \sigma^2 S_t^2 = 0};\\ &\Delta,\text{ by comparing the volatilities}:\,\boxed{\Delta_t = \partial_s V(t,S_t)}. \end{aligned} \]

2 Price Dynamics in the Black–Scholes Market

2.1 Black Scholes Market

In the Black–Scholes model, the market is assumed to consist of two assets: a risky asset with price process \((S_t)_{t\geq 0}\) and the money-market account \((B_t)_{t\geq 0}\).

Since it is a riskless asset, the money-market account is assumed to grow at the risk-free rate \[ dB_t = r B_t\,dt,\qquad t\geq 0, \quad \text{ so that }\quad B_t=B_0e^{rt}. \]

The risky asset’s price is assumed to follow a geometric Brownian motion \[ dS_t = \mu S_t dt+\sigma S_t dW_t, \quad \text{ so that }\quad S_t = S_0 e^{\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}2\right) t+\sigma W_t}\] where

  • \(\mu\in \mathbb R\) is the expected rate of return of the stock;
  • \(\sigma>0\) is its volatility;
  • \(W_t\) is a Brownian motion.

2.2 Assumptions in the Black-Scholes model

There are several idealized assumptions in the Black–Scholes model:

  • trading is possible continuously in time;
  • arbitrary fractions of the stock can be bought or sold;
  • short selling is allowed;
  • arbitrary amounts of money can be invested or borrowed at the same risk-free rate \(r\);
  • there are no transaction costs or taxes;
  • the stock pays no dividends;
  • the parameters \(\mu\), \(\sigma\), and \(r\) are constant.

These assumptions are clearly idealizations. Their purpose is to isolate the central mechanism behind the Black–Scholes model: continuous dynamic hedging.

2.3 Self-financing Portfolios

Practically, a portfolio is self-financing, its value changes only because

1. the stock position changes in value; and 2. the cash position earns interest.

Formally, this leads to the following definition.

Definition (Self-financing Portfolio).

A portfolio with value process \((\Pi_t)_{t\geq 0}\) in the Black-Scholes market is called self-financing if its value satisfies \[ d\Pi_t = \Delta_t\,dS_t + r(\Pi_t-\Delta_tS_t)\,dt. \]

The question is now whether we can choose \(\Delta_t\) in such a way that this self-financing portfolio exactly reproduces the payoff of the option.

3 The Black-Scholes Formula

3.1 The Black-Scholes formula

Theorem (The Black–Scholes formula).

Define \[ d_1(t,s) = \frac{ \ln(s/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)(T-t) }{ \sigma\sqrt{T-t} }, \quad \text{ and } d_2(t,s) = d_1(t,s)-\sigma\sqrt{T-t}. \] Let \(N\) denote the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable.

If there exist are no arbitrage opportunities in the Black-Scholes market, a european put and call option can be perfectly replicated by a hedging portfolio. The corresponding price and number of shares in the replicating portfolio are \[ \boxed{ P_t = Ke^{-r(T-t)}N\!\left(-d_2(t,S_t)\right) - S_tN\!\left(-d_1(t,S_t)\right). } \quad \text{ and }\quad \boxed{ \Delta_t = N\!\left(d_1(t,S_t)\right)-1. } \] for a put option with strike \(K\) and maturity time \(T\) at time \(t<T\). For the analogous call option, they are \[ \boxed{ C_t = S_tN\!\left(d_1(t,S_t)\right) - Ke^{-r(T-t)}N\!\left(d_2(t,S_t)\right), } \quad \text{ and }\quad \boxed{ \Delta_t^{\mathrm{call}} = N\!\left(d_1(t,S_t)\right). } \]

3.2 Proof of the Black-Scholes formula

The value invested in the money-market account is \[ B_t=\Pi_t-\Delta_tS_t. \]

Substituting the stock-price dynamics into the self-financing formula ?@eq-SFprop gives

\[ \begin{aligned} d\Pi_t &= \Delta_t \left( \mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t \right) + r(\Pi_t-\Delta_tS_t)\,dt \\ &= \left[ r\Pi_t + \Delta_t(\mu-r)S_t \right]dt + \Delta_t\sigma S_t\,dW_t. \end{aligned} \]

Notice the three ingredients:

  • \(r\Pi_t\,dt\): the return the portfolio would earn at the risk-free rate;
  • \(\Delta_t(\mu-r)S_t\,dt\): the extra expected return obtained from holding the stock rather than cash;
  • \(\Delta_t\sigma S_t\,dW_t\): the random change caused by the stock-price shock.

The last term is the one that matters for hedging.

Let

\[ p(t,s) \]

denote the value at time \(t\) of our European put when the stock price is \(s\).

The actual value of the put at time \(t\) is therefore the random variable

\[ P_t=p(t,S_t). \]

At maturity there is no ambiguity about its value:

\[ p(T,s)=(K-s)^+. \]

Before maturity, however, \(S_t\) is random, and hence \(p(t,S_t)\) is random.

We can determine how its value evolves using Itô’s formula. For a sufficiently smooth function \(p\),

\[ dp(t,S_t) = p_t(t,S_t)\,dt + p_s(t,S_t)\,dS_t + \frac12p_{ss}(t,S_t)(dS_t)^2. \]

Because

\[ (dS_t)^2 = \sigma^2S_t^2\,dt, \]

we obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} dp(t,S_t) &= \left[ p_t + \mu S_t p_s + \frac12\sigma^2S_t^2p_{ss} \right]dt \\ &\qquad + \sigma S_t p_s\,dW_t, \end{aligned} \]

where, to keep the notation readable, all derivatives are evaluated at \((t,S_t)\).

Thus the option itself has a random \(dW_t\) term,

\[ \sigma S_t p_s\,dW_t. \]

Compare this with the random term in the self-financing portfolio,

\[ \Delta_t\sigma S_t\,dW_t. \]

This comparison contains the key idea.

We want the portfolio to reproduce the value of the option, i.e.

\[ \Pi_t=p(t,S_t). \]

For this equality to persist after an infinitesimal stock-price movement, the two positions must have the same exposure to the Brownian shock \(dW_t\).

Therefore, assuming for the moment that \(p\) is differentiable in the price coordinate, we must choose

\[ \Delta_t=p_s(t,S_t). \]

For a put, \(\Delta_t\) is negative. Intuitively, an increase in the stock price makes the right to sell the stock for \(K\) less valuable.

But matching the \(dW_t\) terms is only half the story. The deterministic \(dt\) terms must agree as well.

Using

\[ \Delta_t=p_s(t,S_t) \]

in the portfolio equation gives

\[ d\Pi_t = \left[ r\Pi_t + (\mu-r)S_tp_s \right]dt + \sigma S_tp_s\,dW_t. \]

The option evolves according to

\[ dp(t,S_t) = \left[ p_t + \mu S_tp_s + \frac12\sigma^2S_t^2p_{ss} \right]dt + \sigma S_tp_s\,dW_t. \]

If \(\Pi_t=p(t,S_t)\), their drift terms must also be equal:

\[ rp + (\mu-r)S_tp_s = p_t + \mu S_tp_s + \frac12\sigma^2S_t^2p_{ss}. \]

Now something remarkable happens.

The term involving the expected return \(\mu\) cancels. After rearranging,

\[ p_t(t,s) + rs\,p_s(t,s) + \frac12\sigma^2s^2p_{ss}(t,s) = rp(t,s). \]

Equivalently,

\[ p_t + rs\,p_s + \frac12\sigma^2s^2p_{ss} -rp = 0. \]

This is the Black–Scholes–Merton partial differential equation.

The expected return \(\mu\) of the stock has disappeared.

This is not because we assumed that \(\mu=r\). We did not.

It disappeared because the Delta hedge made the option and the replicating portfolio have exactly the same exposure to the random stock-price shock. Once that source of risk has been eliminated, no-arbitrage determines the remaining rate of return.

This answers the question with which we started the chapter.

We do not need to predict whether the stock is likely to rise or fall. We need to understand how strongly the option reacts when the stock moves and continuously adjust the stock position accordingly.

Suppose that we can find a function \(p(t,s)\) satisfying

\[ p_t + rs\,p_s + \frac12\sigma^2s^2p_{ss} = rp \]

for \(0\leq t<T\), together with the terminal condition

\[ p(T,s)=(K-s)^+. \]

Start with initial capital

\[ \Pi_0=p(0,S_0) \]

and at every time use the strategy

\[ \Delta_t=p_s(t,S_t). \]

The calculation above shows that the portfolio and the option then have identical infinitesimal changes:

\[ d\Pi_t=dp(t,S_t). \]

Since they also have the same initial value,

\[ \Pi_t=p(t,S_t) \]

for all \(t\).

In particular, at maturity,

\[ \Pi_T = p(T,S_T) = (K-S_T)^+. \]

The terminal put payoff has therefore been replicated.

Under the assumptions of the model, this is not an approximation: it holds for every possible stock-price path.

Consequently,

\[ \text{option price} = \text{cost of the replicating portfolio}. \]

Any other price would permit an arbitrage.

The stochastic problem has now become a deterministic one.

The Black–Scholes equation

\[ p_t + rs\,p_s + \frac12\sigma^2s^2p_{ss} = rp \]

contains no Brownian motion and no expected stock return. Once the variables \(t\) and \(s\) are specified, it is simply a partial differential equation.

For a European option, the payoff at maturity provides the terminal condition.

For a call,

\[ c(T,s)=(s-K)^+, \]

whereas for a put,

\[ p(T,s)=(K-s)^+. \]

For the put it is also natural that

\[ p(t,s)\longrightarrow 0 \qquad\text{as }s\longrightarrow\infty, \]

while if the stock price is zero,

\[ p(t,0)=Ke^{-r(T-t)}. \]

The PDE together with these conditions determines the option value.

We will not derive the analytic PDE solution here. Instead, we state it and verify its implications.

Let

\[ \tau=T-t \]

denote the time remaining until maturity and define

\[ d_1 = \frac{ \ln(s/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau }{ \sigma\sqrt{\tau} }, \]

and

\[ d_2 = d_1-\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = \frac{ \ln(s/K) + \left(r-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\tau }{ \sigma\sqrt{\tau} }. \]

Let \(N\) denote the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable.

Then the Black–Scholes value of a European call is

\[ c(t,s) = sN(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2). \]

The European put price is

\[ p(t,s) = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) - sN(-d_1). \]

We can now return to the rule used at the beginning of the chapter.

Differentiate the put price with respect to \(s\):

\[ \Delta_{\text{put}} = p_s(t,s). \]

The result is

\[ \Delta_{\text{put}} = N(d_1)-1 = -N(-d_1). \]

Substituting the expression for \(d_1\) gives

\[ \Delta_{\text{put}} = N\!\left( \frac{ \ln(s/K) + \left(r+\frac12\sigma^2\right)(T-t) }{ \sigma\sqrt{T-t} } \right)-1. \]

This is precisely the rule that we used at the beginning of the chapter.

It was not an arbitrary formula. It is the number of shares required by the replicating portfolio.

For a European put,

\[ -1<\Delta_{\text{put}}<0. \]

Its interpretation is particularly clear in the limiting cases:

  • if the put is far out of the money, \(\Delta\) is close to \(0\);
  • if the put is far in the money, \(\Delta\) is close to \(-1\);
  • near the strike, Delta lies between these two extremes and changes rapidly as the stock moves.

Thus a put that is almost certain to expire in the money behaves approximately like a short position in one share of stock.
